The Conference

About the Conference

As climate change intensifies and environmental disasters multiply, population displacement has become one of the major challenges facing the international community. Droughts, floods, desertification and rising sea levels all call into question the legal protection of the populations concerned.

International refugee law, based primarily on the 1951 Geneva Convention, does not recognise environmental causes as an autonomous ground for refugee status. The adaptation of existing mechanisms must therefore be rethought, and the contribution of complementary protection — drawn from international human rights law, regional instruments and international environmental law — carefully examined.

Hosted by the Faculty of Legal and Political Sciences of Tangier, this three-day conference brings together academics, researchers and specialists in international law, human rights and the environment, with particular attention to African and Mediterranean perspectives.

Central research question
To what extent can international refugee law provide effective protection to persons displaced by climate change and environmental degradation, and what legal developments are necessary to overcome the limitations of the existing international protection framework?

Objectives of the conference

01

Deepening reflection on the limits of international refugee law

Analyse the capacity of the current framework, notably the 1951 Geneva Convention, to respond to climate and environmental displacement, and examine the scope for an evolutionary interpretation of existing instruments.

02

Examining complementary protection mechanisms

Study the contributions of international human rights law, migration law, international environmental law and regional instruments to the protection of displaced persons.

03

Contributing to the debate on the evolution of the international legal framework

Reflect on adapting refugee law and on new instruments, in light of the Limoges Appeal (2005) and the draft conventions of the International Centre of Comparative Environmental Law (CIDCE).

04

Strengthening national and international scientific cooperation

Foster exchange between academics, researchers, scientific institutions and specialised organisations, and develop lasting collaborations on climate displacement and fundamental rights.

05

Highlighting African and Mediterranean perspectives

Pay particular attention to the legal and institutional challenges of the African and Mediterranean regions, encouraging dialogue between regional experiences and universal protection mechanisms.

06

Formulating scientific and legal recommendations

At the close of the proceedings, identify avenues for reflection and proposals to inform academic and institutional debates on the future protection of displaced persons.

Scientific Framework

Four Scientific Themes

The conference is structured around four themes, examined over three days in six scientific sessions.

01
Theme 1 · Day One

The Legal Construction of Climate-Induced Migration: From the Emergence of a Phenomenon to Its Normative Qualification

Qualification of environmental displacement, the distinction between voluntary migration and forced displacement, and debates on the legal recognition of climate refugees.

02
Theme 2 · Day One

International Refugee Law and Climate Displacement: Between the Rigidity of the Conventional Framework and Interpretative Potential

Limits of the 1951 Geneva Convention, evolutionary interpretation of the refugee definition and prospects for reform — with particular attention to the 2005 Limoges Appeal.

03
Theme 3 · Day Two

Protecting Climate-Displaced Persons Through Complementary Legal Regimes

International human rights law, migration law, international environmental law and complementary protection; African regional instruments, European experiences, and challenges in the African and Mediterranean regions.

04
Theme 4 · Day Three

Towards a Renewed Legal Governance of Climate Mobility

New international instruments, legal and institutional responsibilities of States and international organisations, international solidarity and climate justice, reform of refugee law or a dedicated international convention.

Dr Gabriel AJABU MASTAKI

The 1969 Organization of African Unity Refugee Convention and the Potential Application of the Expanded Refugee Definition to Environmental and Climate Displacement.

Abstract

The absence of any reference to environmental factors in the refugee definition established by the 1951 Geneva Convention generally leads to a search for legal grounds for the protection of persons displaced by climate change outside the framework of international refugee law. However, this approach warrants reconsideration in light of African regional law. By extending refugee status to persons compelled to leave their country due, inter alia, to events seriously disturbing public order, Article I(2) of the 1969 OAU Refugee Convention introduces a criterion whose scope does not necessarily appear to be confined to the political and security circumstances that motivated its adoption.

The difficulty lies less in the absence of an explicit reference to environmental phenomena than in the conditions under which their consequences may be legally characterised as events seriously disturbing public order. While a disaster or the gradual degradation of the environment cannot, in itself, automatically confer refugee status, the resulting disruption of the collective conditions of existence could, in certain circumstances, fall within the scope of this provision.

Drawing on the rules of treaty interpretation, UNHCR guidance, and the practice of African States, this paper seeks to examine the conditions under which the expanded refugee definition may apply to environmental and climate-related displacement. The analysis will focus primarily on the threshold of severity required for a disturbance of public order, the relationship between such a disturbance and the involuntary nature of displacement, and the difficulties involved in assessing these elements in situations where multiple drivers of human mobility intersect. Ultimately, the paper aims to assess the extent to which the limitations of protection under the Convention arise from the content of the legal provision itself or from the conditions governing its interpretation and application by African States.

About the speaker

Dr Gabriel Ajabu Mastaki, a national of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, holds a PhD in Law from the University of Burundi, prepared under joint supervision with UCLouvain and Université Paris Cité. He is currently completing a second doctoral thesis in Legal Sciences at UCLouvain.

He is a researcher at the Centre Charles De Visscher pour le droit international et européen (CeDIE), within the Équipe droits et migrations (EDEM), UCLouvain, Belgium.

His research interests include international human rights law, international environmental law, international migration law and the legal protection of persons displaced in the context of climate change.
